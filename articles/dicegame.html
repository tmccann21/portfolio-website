Dice Game

A statistical analysis of the winning strategy in Dice Game· 2026

My friends and I have a game we like to play which we've affectionately come to refer to as Dice Game. It was introduced as 10 000; I've learned it can also be called Farkle or Zilch, but to me it will only be Dice Game. The rules are:

  • You win the game by getting to 10 000 points1
  • Each turn you must score one or more dice by setting them aside and adding to your turn's score
    • 5s are worth 50 each
    • 1s are worth 100 each
    • three of a kind is worth 100 × face number
    • three 1s is worth 1000
    • groups double for each additional die
    • 1 2 3 4 5 6 is worth 1500
  • If you score all six dice, you can start over and continue adding to your turn's score
  • If you roll any number of dice and none of them score, you've busted and your turn ends
  • You bank points by choosing to stop rolling before you've busted. When you do this, you add your turn's score to your total score.2
  • Piggybacking. After you bank your points, the next player can choose to continue rolling from where you left off. They inherit your turn's score and any dice remaining. They must roll at least once - they cannot simply take your points and pass. You must be on the board to piggyback.
footnotes
  1. in order to win, you must get exactly 10 000 points. If the dice you roll bring your score over 10 000, you bust. You may choose to keep a scoring subset, so long as it does not exceed 10 000
  2. in order to bank points, you must first be on the board. To get on the board, you must bank 750 points in a single turn; until then you must keep rolling.

Perfect Play vs. Recursion

Without the re-rolling rule, calculating the EV for any decision is straightforward. Take the case where we've scored five dice and have one left to roll. There's a \(\frac{2}{3}\) chance we lose our current total (roll a 2, 3, 4, or 6), a \(\frac{1}{6}\) chance we add 50, and a \(\frac{1}{6}\) chance we add 100. Let \(v(n)\) denote the expected gain from rolling \(n\) dice (the weighted upside). The net expected value of rolling, \(ev(1) = v(1) - score\), is positive when the upside outweighs what you'd forfeit by busting.

With the re-roll rule however, we introduce a recursive term. Now rolling a one would net us 100 points for the 1, plus the value of \(v(6)\), the weighted upside of rolling all six dice again. My initial fear was that the only way to solve this was unrolling the recursion out until we'd exceed 10 000 points, therefore ending the game.

Instead, there's a much more elegant solution - ignore it. We express \(v(1)\) in terms of a constant + \(v(6)\), which we then use to express \(v(2)\) as the upside of scoring both dice + \(v(6)\) and the upside of scoring 1 dice + \(v(1)\) (which we've just defined as a constant + a linear scaling of \(v(6)\)). We can then expand and simplify to once again get a constant + a linear scaling of \(v(6)\). We repeat this for \(v(3)\) through \(v(6)\) which in the end gives us: $$\begin{aligned} v(6) &= \epsilon + \lambda\, v(6) \\ v(6) &= \frac{\epsilon}{1-\lambda} \end{aligned}$$ Which solves directly, since we know \(\lambda\) and \(\epsilon\). Plugging back into each \(v(x)\) gives the expected upside per die count. \(v(1)\) through \(v(4)\) were solved by hand; beyond that the permutations grow enough that a numerical approach is easier, but more on that shortly.

Starting Small

The first case: five dice scored, one remaining. The upside is the chance of scoring the last die plus the value of re-rolling all six. Only two faces score here, a one or a five. $$ \begin{aligned} v(1) &= \frac{50 + v(6)}{6} + \frac{100 + v(6)}{6} \\ &= \frac{150 + 2v(6)}{6} \\ &= 25 + \frac{v(6)}{3} \end{aligned} $$ With this in hand, we can tackle the four dice scored, two to roll case. Substituting our value for \(v(1)\) at the end. $$\begin{aligned} v(2) &= \frac{100 + v(6)}{6^2} &&\text{roll two 5s + re-roll} \\ &+ \frac{2(150 + v(6))}{6^2} &&\text{roll one 1, one 5 + re-roll} \\ &+ \frac{200 + v(6)}{6^2} &&\text{roll two 1s + re-roll} \\ &+ \frac{8(50 + v(1))}{6^2} &&\text{roll one 5 + } v(1) \\ &+ \frac{8(100 + v(1))}{6^2} &&\text{roll one 1 + } v(1) \\[10pt] &= \frac{2200}{36} + \frac{7\,v(6)}{27} &&\text{substitute } v(1)\text{, simplify} \end{aligned}$$ For \(v(3)\), the process is the same; we multiply each scoring permutation by its likelihood. $$\begin{aligned} v(3) &= \frac{4350 + 12\,v(6)}{6^3} &&\text{all 3 score + re-roll} \\ &+ \frac{7200 + 48\,v(1)}{6^3} &&\text{2 score + } v(1) \\ &+ \frac{7200 + 96\,v(2)}{6^3} &&\text{1 scores + } v(2) \\[10pt] &= \frac{116175}{972} + \frac{238\,v(6)}{972} \end{aligned}$$

The Greedy Assumption

Until now we've assumed the greedy approach is always best. The four dice case is the first place that breaks down. Roll \((1,5,n_1,n_2)\): do we keep both the 1 and 5, or just the 1? For the greedy choice to be optimal we need \(v(3) - v(2) < 50\). $$\begin{aligned} v(3) - v(2) &= \left(\frac{116175}{972} + \frac{238\,v(6)}{972}\right) - \left(\frac{2200}{36} + \frac{7\,v(6)}{27}\right) \\ &= \frac{116175 - 59400}{972} + \frac{238 - 252}{972}v(6) \\ &= \frac{56775 - 14\,v(6)}{972} \end{aligned}$$ The greedy choice holds only when \(v(6) > 8175/14 \approx 584\). We don't know \(v(6)\) yet, so we carry the assumption forward and switch to a computational solver.

Computational Solution

Conditional choices (and increasingly ugly fractions) make solving by hand a much more tedious proposition. In order to continue solving Dice Game, we need to show that across all possible decisions we've found the optimal solution. In this case, we need to solve for \(v(6)\) following both the greedy and non-greedy paths until a numerically stable solution is found. We can imagine that as the decision space gets bigger with more dice to roll this leads to a combinatorial explosion of the number of outcomes to explore. To make this tractable, we'll instead write a computational solver to evaluate solutions much more quickly. In pseudocode, the algorithm works like this: 

V[6] = 0

repeat until |V[6] - V[6]_prev| < ε:
    V[1..3] = closed-form expressions in terms of V[6]

    for n in {4, 5, 6}:
        for each outcome in all 6^n rolls:
            best_ev = 0
            for each valid keep strategy:
                remaining = n - dice kept
                ev = score(kept) + (V[6] if remaining == 0 else V[remaining])
                best_ev = max(best_ev, ev)
        V[n] = mean(best_ev) over all outcomes

Since the 6-dice case only has \(6^6\) possible enumerations, this search runs and converges quickly. The following are our expected gains from rolling \(n\) dice, including the possibility of resetting all six dice.

The first result which stands out from this is of course that \(v(2) < v(1)\). This makes some sense intuitively; there's not many points to earn with just two dice so the lower probability of getting to a re-roll dominates. We can solve for the exact value of \(v(6)\) at which two dice expects to score less than one dice:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{2200}{36} + \frac{7\,v(6)}{27} &< 25 + \frac{v(6)}{3} \\[6pt] \frac{1300}{36} &< \frac{2\,v(6)}{27} \\[6pt] v(6) &> \frac{35100}{72} = 487.5 \end{aligned}$$

This demonstrates \(v(2) < v(1)\) in any scenario where \(v(6) > 487.5\)

Non-Greedy Cases

Our earlier conditional that the greedy choice is optimal in the four-dice case when \(v(6) > 584\) is satisfied, but this was only the first real non-greedy option we had to evaluate. The options in the 5 and 6-dice cases are much more interesting, and indeed with our computed value of \(v(6)\) we encounter the first cases where the non-greedy option yields a higher expected value.

Consider the scenario where a player has five dice to roll. They roll two fives, and three non-scoring dice. We faced similar decisions before with four dice, but we've since run the computational solver to get the actual value of \(v(6)\), so we can now evaluate this decision directly. $$\begin{aligned} 50 + v(4) &> 100 + v(3) \\ v(4) - v(3) &> 50 \\ 96 &> 50 \end{aligned}$$ In this case, it's better to keep one five and re-roll four dice over keeping two fives and re-rolling three. The case with a one and a five reduces to the same conditional, but the greedy decision still narrowly holds for the case with two ones as seen by the following contradiction. $$\begin{aligned} 100 + v(4) &> 200 + v(3) \\ v(4) - v(3) &> 100 \\ 96 &> 100 \end{aligned}$$

With six dice, we opt for the non-greedy case any time we roll exactly two scoring dice. In these cases \(v(5) - v(4) = 145\) is greater than any single dice would add so it's always optimal to re-roll five dice over four.

The final edge case to consider is rolling three twos, one one and two non-scoring dice. Our choices are keep three twos, keep the one, or keep everything. By keeping everything we expect \(300 + v(2) = 550\). Keeping just the twos we expect \(200 + v(3) = 498 \) and keeping the one we expect \(100 + v(5) = 639\), our winning decision. This does not hold for any groups of higher value; we see with triple threes that the greedy case is now worth \(400 + v(2) = 650\), narrowly beating out the non-greedy option.

How to Win At Dice Game

Understanding the expected score for rolling a number of dice, and analyzing cases where the player should be non-greedy build the foundation for perfect play, but they don't answer the question of What should I do on my turn? To know that, we need one more thing: the probability of busting (scoring no dice) for any number of dice. Applied to banking, this is the same formula from earlier: \(ev(n) = v(n) - score \cdot p_\text{bust}(n)\). Rolling gives upside \(v(n)\); busting costs your current score with probability \(p_\text{bust}(n)\). Piggybacking

The other key decision a player of Dice Game needs to make is whether to piggyback or start fresh. At this moment, we don't have the choice of stopping to bank our points, so instead we compare the expected value of each. Starting fresh gives you \(v(6) = 730\) in expectation. With inherited score \(S\) and \(n\) dice, piggybacking yields \((1 - p_\text{bust}(n)) \cdot S + v(n)\) in expectation; we prefer it whenever \(S > (v(6) - v(n)) / (1 - p_\text{bust}(n))\). Summary

So far we've covered the optimal strategy for a single player's turn in a vacuum in Dice Game. In reality, the game is much more complex and truly optimal play involves considering not just your position but the positions of your opponents. The key ideas here should go pretty far in beating your friends at Dice Game/Farkle/Zilch, but a complete analysis of perfect play requires digging deeper into strategies with multiple players at different stages in the game. In a follow up, I'd like to simulate a tournament of bots playing against each other with different strategies and risk levels. For now, remember: one die is better than two, bank when you've got good points, and piggyback when your friend hands you too many dice.
